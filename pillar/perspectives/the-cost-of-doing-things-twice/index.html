The cost of doing things twice

Some of the most expensive operational problems are the ones we stop noticing. Repeated work, delayed decisions and unnecessary steps can quietly affect everything from staff productivity to the resident experience.

Melanie Graham Prock
President, Pillar Communities
Not yet published / 2 min read

One of the most expensive things in property management rarely shows up as its own line item. It's the cost of doing things twice.

A question that gets passed between departments. A maintenance issue that could have been prevented. A leasing opportunity that sits too long without follow up. A decision that takes three approvals when one would do.

None of these feel expensive in the moment. Each one is a few minutes, a second visit, an extra email. But they repeat every day at every property, and over a year they add up to real money and real frustration for the people doing the work.

Where it shows up

In my experience it collects in the same few places. The same resident information typed into more than one system. A work order that gets closed, then reopened because the root cause was never fixed. A prospect who asked a question on Tuesday and heard back on Friday, after touring somewhere else. Lease paperwork that has to be corrected after it was already signed.

When people responded to this on LinkedIn, the comments raised these same points: duplicate data entry, slow leasing follow up and lease administration.

Why it hides

Rework is spread across too many budgets to be anyone's line item. It looks like normal payroll, normal turn costs and normal turnover. That is why it survives.

If you cannot see it in the numbers, you have to go looking for it in the work itself.

Where I would look first

Ask an onsite team where they enter the same thing twice. They will know immediately. Look at how many approvals a routine decision needs, and whether each one changes the outcome. Pull a month of reopened work orders. Check how long a new lead waits for a real answer.

Technology can certainly help. But I've learned that before adding another system, it's worth asking whether the process itself needs to change. Often the answer is giving the people closest to the resident the authority to finish the job the first time.

Executive summary

Duplicate entry, reopened work orders, slow lead follow up and extra approvals rarely appear as a cost line, but they raise payroll, vacancy and turnover. Finding them means asking onsite teams, not reading reports.
